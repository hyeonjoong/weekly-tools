# Non-contact respiration-guided acoustic stimulation for chronic insomnia (SERENE): a randomised sham-controlled trial

## Abstract

Background: Slow breathing has been proposed to raise parasympathetic tone and deepen slow-wave sleep, but device-based evidence in chronic insomnia remains limited.

Methods: Adults with chronic insomnia were randomised to eight weeks of respiration-guided acoustic stimulation or to a sham device. The primary outcome was the change in the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) at week 8.

Results: The mean ISI decreased by 5.2 points (SD 3.1) in the active arm and by 2.1 points (SD 2.8) in the sham arm.

Conclusions: Respiration-guided acoustic stimulation reduced insomnia severity over eight weeks in this single-centre trial.

## Introduction

Chronic insomnia affects roughly one in ten adults and is associated with impaired daytime function, reduced work productivity and increased cardiovascular risk.

Paced breathing at about six breaths per minute increases respiratory sinus arrhythmia and shifts cardiac autonomic balance toward vagal predominance. Whether an unattended bedside device can reproduce this effect during natural sleep is unknown.

The SERENE trial tested whether a non-contact device that detects respiration and delivers acoustic cues timed to the exhalation phase improves insomnia severity compared with a sham device.

## Methods

### Participants

Adults aged 20 to 65 years meeting DSM-5 criteria for chronic insomnia disorder were recruited from two sleep clinics between March and October 2025.

### Randomisation and masking

Participants were assigned 1:1 by a computer-generated sequence with block sizes of four. Both arms received an identical-looking bedside unit; the sham unit played a fixed pink-noise track unrelated to respiration. The sham unit was acoustically matched to the active unit in loudness and spectral profile, and neither participants nor outcome assessors were informed which unit they handled.

### Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in ISI from baseline to week 8. Secondary outcomes were RMSSD during the first sleep hour, slow-wave activity from single-channel EEG, and sleep-diary total sleep time.

### Statistical analysis

Between-arm differences were analysed with a linear mixed model including arm, visit and their interaction, with participant as a random intercept. Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Assuming a between-group difference of 3.0 points on the ISI with an SD of 4.5, 45 participants per arm provide 80% power at a two-sided alpha of 0.05.

## Results

Of the 84 randomised participants, 78 completed the week-8 assessment, comprising 40 of 42 in the active arm and 38 of 42 in the sham arm (Table 2).

The mean ISI decreased by 5.8 points (SD 3.4) in the active arm and by 2.1 points (SD 2.8) in the sham arm, giving a between-arm difference of 3.7 points (95% CI 2.1 to 5.3).

RMSSD during the first hour of sleep increased by 6.4 ms in the active arm and by 1.2 ms in the sham arm.

Slow-wave activity in the first two NREM cycles was numerically higher in the active arm, but the between-arm contrast was prespecified as exploratory and is reported without inferential testing.

## Discussion

The magnitude of ISI improvement is comparable with that reported for brief behavioural interventions, while requiring no scheduled clinician contact.

This trial has limitations. Polysomnography was performed only at baseline, so the EEG-derived measures rely on a single-channel montage. Because both clinics serve an urban adult population, the findings may not generalise to older adults or to patients with untreated sleep apnoea.
